Row House Shared Sewer Laterals In York: What To Do Before And During A Backup

If you own a row house in York's historic core, there's a decent chance your sewer lateral, the line that carries wastewater from your house to the main sewer, is shared with the house next door. This is a genuinely common setup in older row-house construction. Here's how to find out before you have a problem, and exactly what to do when a backup happens.

Why Shared Laterals Exist

When York's historic row houses were built, running an individual lateral for every unit wasn't always standard practice. Instead, two or more adjoining properties were connected to a single lateral line running out to the main sewer. That setup has persisted in a lot of the historic core ever since, and it's rarely disclosed clearly at the point of sale.

Step 1: Find Out Now, Before You Have A Backup

  1. Check your property's sewer connection permit or tap record, which is sometimes available through York City or York County property records, either online or by request
  2. Ask your immediate neighbors directly whether they know their line is shared. Long-time owners on the block often know even when it's not in any paperwork
  3. Look for a single cleanout access point that seems to serve more than one property, often near the property line or in a shared side yard
  4. If you're buying a row house, ask the seller directly and get it in writing if they confirm a shared lateral, since it affects your maintenance responsibility going forward

What It Means When Your Drain Backs Up

If your basement floor drain or a lower fixture backs up and you share a lateral, the clog might not even be on your property. It could be tree roots, debris, or a blockage originating at your neighbor's connection point, still backing up into your basement because the shared line has nowhere else to go.

Step 2: What To Do The Moment You Notice A Backup

  1. Stop running any water in your house immediately: no flushing, no laundry, no dishwasher. Every bit of added water makes the backup worse and pushes contaminated water further into your home
  2. If it's safe to do so, check whether the backup is isolated to your basement drain or if you can tell your neighbor is experiencing the same thing at the same time, which strongly suggests a shared-line issue rather than something isolated to your plumbing
  3. Avoid direct contact with the backed-up water, since sewage backup carries real contamination risk. Keep children and pets away from the area
  4. Call for a camera inspection rather than guessing. A camera run down the line can usually pinpoint whether the blockage is on your side of the shared connection or your neighbor's

Who's Responsible For The Fix

This is genuinely one of the murkier parts of owning a shared-lateral property, and it varies by the specific agreement, deed language, or municipal code that applies to your block. In practice, many neighbors split the cost of clearing a shared line when the blockage's exact origin point can't be cleanly isolated to one property, since a camera inspection confirms the shared responsibility even if it can locate roughly where the clog sits. If a clear point of origin is identified on one property's side of the connection, that owner is more commonly expected to cover the cost. Getting this in writing with your neighbor before there's ever a problem, even an informal agreement about splitting future maintenance costs, saves a lot of friction later.

Preventing The Next One

  • If your line has a known root intrusion history, an annual or biannual hydro jetting keeps the pipe walls clear rather than waiting for the next backup
  • Coordinate with your neighbor on a shared maintenance schedule rather than each of you calling separately after the next backup, which tends to cost both of you more over time
  • Avoid flushing wipes, grease, or paper towels, which compound faster in an older, already-narrower shared line than in a modern individual lateral
